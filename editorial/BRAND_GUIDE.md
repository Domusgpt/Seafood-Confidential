# Seafood Confidential brand guide

Version 0.1 · 17 November 2026 · Working direction for founder review

## The identity

**Seafood Confidential**  
**Seafood, from the people who know it.**

Descriptor: Fisheries, aquaculture, trade, and the people behind the food.

Seafood Confidential is a practitioner-led publication about the decisions, working lives, businesses, science, policy, and food cultures that connect seafood production to the table. Paul Phillips and his cofounder bring decades of seafood experience. The publication turns that experience, and the knowledge of invited contributors, into reporting and conversations that are useful to the trade and understandable to interested readers beyond it.

The name promises candor and access. Actual source confidentiality is a separate arrangement. An ordinary pitch form should never imply that it provides a protected channel for sensitive disclosures.

## Audience and promise

The first audience is seafood buyers, suppliers, fishers, farmers, processors, chefs, and serious industry followers. An article should still be readable by someone encountering its subject for the first time. Explain the trade term that matters, then use it accurately.

The editorial promise is simple: **help readers understand a real seafood decision, the people affected, and the evidence behind it.**

The distinguishing asset is working knowledge made public: how a buyer judges a shipment, how a farmer makes a production decision, how a rule changes a working day, how a market develops, and how those choices reach the plate. Personal access starts the story; reporting and clear attribution make it trustworthy.

### About introduction

Seafood is a working world: boats and farms, buying desks and cold stores, kitchens and coastal communities. Its choices reach far beyond the fish counter.

Seafood Confidential was founded by Paul Phillips and a fellow seafood industry veteran to make that world easier to understand. We bring decades of experience to the conversation and invite people across fisheries, aquaculture, trade, science, policy, and food to add their own knowledge.

We publish reported features, practical explainers, first-person accounts, and interviews. A writer's perspective should be clear, commercial relationships should be disclosed, and readers should be able to follow the evidence behind a claim. When we make a material mistake, we correct the record.

If you have a story, a question, or a perspective the seafood world should hear, we would like to hear from you through our editorial contact page.

## Editorial vocabulary

Use **fishery** for the particular activity, population, or management context being discussed; identify place and species when the distinction matters. Use **aquaculture** broadly enough to include marine and freshwater production, shellfish, finfish, and aquatic plants where relevant. Use **seafood** as the publication's familiar umbrella, while explaining a narrower meaning when a source uses one.

Describe wild and farmed systems with comparable attention to place, method, economics, and evidence. Avoid treating either category as a complete answer to a sourcing question.

Prefer concrete language: buyer, harvester, farmer, processor, freight cost, yield, effective date, interview, source. Explain abbreviations on first use. Use the units and currencies that make the story intelligible; display conversions as dated calculations with assumptions.

The voice is direct, curious, warm, and occasionally wry. Humor can come from experience and observation. Do not let it obscure a serious allegation, a safety claim, or the meaning of a quotation.

## Working Waterfront visual system

The visual direction is an independent trade journal with maritime character. It should feel credible on a buying desk and inviting at a kitchen table.

| Token | Color | Intended use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ink | `#102B3A` | Masthead, primary text, dark covers, navigation |
| Salt | `#F3EFE5` | Main background, paper surfaces, light text on ink |
| Sea glass | `#7FA59A` | Secondary panels, rules, charts, quiet accents |
| Rust | `#B64D35` | Small emphasis, selected labels, calls to action after contrast testing |

Use ink on salt for ordinary text. Sea glass is a surface or accent color rather than the default color of small text. Check text and controls against the final backgrounds before release; this palette is not a claim that every color pairing is accessible.

Use a strong serif masthead and headlines with a clear sans-serif body and interface. A practical design starting point is a locally hosted editorial serif and a humanist sans, with system fallbacks. Confirm font licensing and loading behavior when the theme is chosen. Keep body text comfortably readable on a phone, use generous line spacing, and avoid text embedded in images when live text will work.

Begin with a typographic wordmark. An optional original SC monogram or restrained wave device can serve small avatars and favicons. Keep the tagline separate enough to remove at very small sizes. The wordmark must work in one color.

### Photography and illustration

Show real people and working environments: decks and ponds, hatcheries and cold stores, fish markets and kitchens, tropical and temperate locations, marine and freshwater production. Give fisheries and aquaculture equal visual standing.

Record permission, credit, location, and whether an image is documentary, illustrative, or generated. A generated concept image can communicate design direction. It should not be presented as a photograph of a reported person, operation, or event.

Crop for the subject and preserve useful context. Confirm that labels, location markers, private documents, and identifying details are appropriate for publication. Use specific captions and useful alternative text.

### Reusable visual assets

Create one article feature layout, one quotation layout, one source-led explainer layout, and one newsletter header. Add podcast artwork only when the pilot is commissioned. Each layout should leave enough space for a legible title, a format label, and a brief credit. Favor consistency over generating a new design for every post.

## Brand boundaries and approvals

Paul is the proposed brand owner. His cofounder is the proposed managing editor; their name and final remit remain to be confirmed. The managing editor can use approved templates and copy. Material changes to the name, tagline, masthead, commercial identity, or editorial promise return to the founders.

The first release is an editorial publication and newsletter. Additional memberships, directories, events, market tools, and other services should be named and introduced when their scope is clear.
